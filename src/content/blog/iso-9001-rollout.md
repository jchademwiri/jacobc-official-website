Certification looks straightforward on paper: a quality manual, a few procedures, an audit. In practice it's a few months of getting the organisation to actually work the way the documentation says it does. The gap between "written down" and "actually followed" is where most rollouts either succeed or quietly stall.

## 1. Gap analysis before you write anything

Start by mapping what already happens against the clauses of the standard, not against a generic template pulled from the internet. Most of the work in a rollout is formalising existing practice, not inventing new process from scratch.

Sit with whoever actually does the work: the person receiving goods, the person handling a customer complaint, the person signing off a job. Their version of the process is the one that needs documenting, because that's the version an auditor will actually see happening on the floor.

## 2. Write procedures people will use, not procedures that look thorough

A procedure document that runs to twelve pages rarely gets read past the first two. Write for the person who has to follow it under time pressure: short, sequenced steps, with the exceptions called out rather than buried in prose.

If a procedure needs a flowchart to explain itself, that's usually a sign the underlying process is more complicated than it needs to be. Simplifying the process is often more valuable than documenting it well.

## 3. Build the records habit before the audit, not for it

Auditors look for records: signed-off management reviews, corrective action logs, training registers, calibration certificates. A policy nobody can point to evidence for is a finding waiting to happen, no matter how well the policy itself is written.

Records only exist consistently if the habit of creating them is built into the daily routine early, months before the audit date, not manufactured retroactively in the final weeks. A retroactively assembled record set is usually obvious to an experienced auditor, and it tends to unravel under a couple of follow-up questions.

## 4. Run an internal audit before the external one

An internal audit, even an informal one, surfaces the gaps while there's still time to close them. Walk through the same clauses an external auditor will check, in the same order, and treat every finding as a gift rather than a failure.

This is also where [project management discipline](/services#project-management) earns its keep: an ISO rollout is a project with a deadline, and the corrective actions from an internal audit need owners and dates the same way any other project task would.

## 5. The certification audit rewards evidence, not intention

By the time the external audit happens, the outcome is mostly already decided by what's been built in the months before it. The audit itself is a verification exercise, not a test you can cram for the night before.

An auditor asking "show me" is really asking whether the system lives in daily operations or only in a folder. Answering that question well is the entire point of the rollout.
